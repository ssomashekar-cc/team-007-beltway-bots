# Recommendations for Sea Turtle Protection and Tourism-Compatible Policies

## Purpose and Basis

This memo recommends practical updates to the sea-turtle lighting provisions supplied for eight Broward County municipalities. It uses Melbourne Beach as a useful example of explicit lighting outcomes, existing-building measures, public-light controls, and nighttime inspections. It does not recommend copying Melbourne Beach's May 1-October 31 nesting season: all eight Broward municipal sources reviewed use March 1-October 31, which should remain the default unless current Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission (FWC) or Florida Department of Environmental Protection (FDEP) guidance supports a change.

These are policy recommendations, not a legal opinion or a finding that the supplied code excerpts are current. Municipal attorneys, code officials, FWC/FDEP, sea-turtle lighting experts, public-safety staff, tourism operators, and acoustic specialists should review any ordinance language before adoption.

## Recommended Shared Standard

Adopt a common minimum standard across municipalities, then retain stronger local protections where they already exist. Make the standard apply to public and private property, new and existing development, temporary construction lighting, events, parking, signs, balconies, windows, and beach access points.

### 1. Define the protected period and hours

Keep March 1-October 31 as the shared nesting season, subject to review by wildlife agencies. Define "nighttime" directly instead of leaving it to interpretation. A practical standard is sunset to sunrise for beach-facing light controls, with a fixed 9:00 p.m. curfew for nonessential exterior lighting during nesting season. This gives restaurants and visitors the early evening while ensuring decorative and promotional lighting is off later. Essential, shielded, turtle-safe lighting may remain on for safety and access.

**Suggested language:**

> "Nesting season" means March 1 through October 31. "Nighttime" means the period from local sunset to local sunrise. During nesting season, nonessential exterior lighting within line of sight of the beach must be extinguished no later than 9:00 p.m. Essential safety and access lighting may remain in use only when it meets the wildlife-lighting standards in this section.

### 2. Regulate the effect of light, not just the fixture label

Prohibit direct, indirect, and cumulative illumination of the beach, dune, or nesting habitat during nighttime in nesting season. Also require that the lamp or reflective fixture surface not be visible from the beach. This closes gaps where a shielded-looking fixture, glass facade, pool, or group of individually dim lights still lights the habitat.

Require a lighting plan for new construction, redevelopment, exterior-light replacement, and material changes to windows or glazing. Plans should show fixture location, mounting height, lamp type and spectrum, shielding, controls, and a nighttime photometric result at the beach/dune edge.

### 3. Specify turtle-compatible fixtures

- Use FWC-certified wildlife lighting where available, with true amber, orange, or red LEDs or another FWC-approved long-wavelength source. A practical specification used in Broward's supplied ordinances is a wavelength above 560 nanometers. Do not treat a yellow filter over a white lamp as equivalent to a true long-wavelength source.
- Require fully shielded, opaque, nonreflective hoods or louvers; light must be directed downward and landward. Prohibit unshielded floodlights, uplights, lasers, and decorative beams that are visible from or illuminate the beach.
- Use the lowest mounting height that safely serves the purpose. For beach-facing parking and paths, use low-profile fixtures with the source no more than 48 inches above grade where feasible. If a taller pole is needed for an identified safety need, require a photometric plan and shielding that prevents beach illumination.
- Limit security lighting to the minimum number and intensity needed. Require downward-directed motion sensors set to turn off within five minutes, rather than merely recommending them.
- For windows and glass doors in line of sight of the beach, require tint or film with inside-to-outside visible-light transmittance of 45 percent or less, or an equivalent exterior shade/screen or interior blackout treatment closed during nighttime. Apply this to upper floors as well as ground floors.
- Shield pool, spa, sign, balcony, stairwell, parking-garage, and construction lighting. Prevent vehicle headlights from reaching the beach with site layout, opaque barriers, or landscaping that does not block turtle nesting or hatchling emergence.

### 4. Make existing-building compliance achievable and enforceable

Do not grandfather harmful lighting indefinitely. Require immediate, no-cost steps at adoption (turn off unnecessary lights, close blinds, re-aim fixtures), compliance for new fixtures immediately, and a documented retrofit schedule for existing fixtures. A reasonable proposed phase-in is one nesting season for simple shields, lamps, timers, and window coverings, and up to three years for capital work, with extensions only for documented hardship and an interim lighting plan. Require compliance when a property changes fixtures, renovates exterior lighting, or replaces glazing.

Require owners of beachfront properties to submit a short compliance checklist or lighting plan. City staff should conduct a nighttime beach survey at least once early in nesting season, repeat after corrective work, and inspect new development before occupancy. Give owners a correction notice for first-time correctable violations; use escalating fines for repeat, deliberate, or high-impact violations. Publish the requirements and a fixture/shielding guide for property managers and contractors.

### 5. Keep tourism active while controlling late-night impacts

The goal should be safe, attractive, low-impact visitor use, not a blanket ban on evening activity. Preserve beach access and ordinary daytime operations. Let beachfront businesses remain open, but direct outdoor activity landward, use certified amber path lighting, close or shade beach-facing glazing after the curfew, and keep nonessential exterior lighting off. Allow approved events through a clear seasonal permit rather than discretionary, undefined exceptions.

**Suggested event language:**

> A special event during nesting season may be approved only with an event lighting and sound plan. Lighting must use shielded, downward-directed wildlife-compatible fixtures and must not directly, indirectly, or cumulatively illuminate the beach or dune. Outdoor amplified sound must end by 9:00 p.m. and must not be audible from the beach or dune from 9:00 p.m. until sunrise. Indoor events may continue if doors and windows facing the beach remain closed and sound is not audible at the beach or dune. Emergency, conservation, and research activities authorized by the appropriate agency are exempt.

The proposed sound rule is a precautionary disturbance standard, not a claim that noise has the same disorienting effect as artificial light. Before setting a numeric decibel limit, cities should measure local background sound and seek acoustic and sea-turtle expert input. Permit conditions can require speakers to face inland, prohibit beach-directed speakers and lasers, set event end times, and require a responsible on-site contact.

### 6. Clarify beach activities

Where a turtle ordinance does not address vehicles, fires, or overnight structures, either add explicit rules or cross-reference the exact beach-use provision that governs them. A shared seasonal baseline could prohibit motor vehicles and fires on nesting beaches from sunset to sunrise, with narrow exceptions for emergency response, authorized research/conservation, and necessary beach maintenance. Require chairs, cabanas, umbrellas, and other temporary items to be removed nightly or securely stacked so they do not trap turtles or block nesting.

## Adoption Checklist

- Confirm the nesting season and any species-specific conditions with FWC/FDEP; retain March 1-October 31 unless current advice supports a change.
- Define sunset, sunrise, nighttime, nesting habitat, direct/indirect/cumulative illumination, essential safety lighting, and special event in each code.
- Adopt common fixture, window, parking, public-lighting, construction, event, and existing-building requirements; maintain more protective local provisions.
- Map beachfront properties and public lights; provide a one-page owner checklist, examples of acceptable shields/fixtures, and a permit-plan template.
- Phase in existing-building work, inspect from the beach at night, and track corrections and repeat violations.
- Report annually on lighting compliance, turtle disorientation/hatchling crawl direction where monitoring is available, nest outcomes, complaints, event permits, visitor use, and safety incidents. Adjust implementation based on results.

## Tourism and Conservation Balance

These recommendations concentrate restrictions on beach-facing light and amplified outdoor sound during the nesting season and late-night hours. They preserve daytime beach use, evening dining and events under permit, indoor nighttime activity, and shielded low-level path and safety lighting. The most important implementation safeguards are predictable hours, clear fixture examples, practical retrofit deadlines, a narrow emergency/safety exception, and consistent inspections. Measure visitor impacts alongside conservation results rather than assuming either outcome.
