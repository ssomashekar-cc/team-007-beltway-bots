# Recommendations for Broward County: Better Sea Turtle Outcomes Without Hurting the Economy

_Based on [economic_impact_analysis.md](economic_impact_analysis.md) and [broward_brevard_sea_turtle_statutory_comparison.md](broward_brevard_sea_turtle_statutory_comparison.md)._

> **Read this first.** These recommendations follow from the patterns in the analysis, not from proof of what works. The analysis compared the two counties side by side and did not measure cause and effect. Its compliance costs are illustrative assumptions, not sourced data.

## What the analysis says about Broward

- **Each nest carries high economic stakes.** About $2.4M of tourism GDP per nest, roughly 74 times Brevard. Blanket restrictions are expensive per nest protected, so targeted measures fit better.
- **The properties that have to comply are concentrated.** About 1,800 address points are within 250 m of the beach, mostly in 6 cities.
- **Compliance costs are small next to tourism.** Even the high cost scenario is about 1.4% of one year's tourism GDP. A 0.1% change in visitor spending is worth about $8M in GDP. Rules that keep the beach experience intact cost little in economic terms.
- **Many beachfront homes are empty.** 41–47% of housing near the beach is vacant or seasonal, compared with 12% countywide. These homes can leave lights on with no one there to switch them off.
- **Visitor numbers are large.** About 3,600 visitors per nest, a big audience for conservation-based tourism.

## Recommendations

| # | Recommendation | Why (from the analysis) | Economic impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Focus lighting help and enforcement on the roughly 1,800 addresses within 250 m of the beach**, starting with Fort Lauderdale, Lauderdale-by-the-Sea, Pompano Beach, Deerfield Beach, Hollywood and Hillsboro Beach. | The properties that matter are few and concentrated. | Low. Full cost-sharing in the mid scenario is about $9M one-time, about 1% of one year's state and local tourism taxes. |
| 2 | **Offer a retrofit cost-share program** (amber LEDs, shielding, window tint). Use state sea turtle grants and municipal funds, and check which local tourism revenue is legally allowed to pay for it. | Owners comply faster when they don't bear the whole cost. | Low. One-time spending that also supports local contractors. |
| 3 | **Target vacant and seasonal homes**: notify owners before March 1, require timers or motion sensors, and hold condo associations and property managers responsible. | 41–47% of beachfront housing is vacant or seasonal. | Very low. It's paperwork and controls, not construction. |
| 4 | **Require turtle-safe lighting in all new beachfront building and redevelopment permits.** | Building it in from the start is much cheaper than retrofitting later. | Very low per project. |
| 5 | **Fix public lighting first**: city streetlights, parks and parking along A1A. | Public lights are under government control and put no burden on businesses. | Paid from public budgets, with no direct cost to tourism. |
| 6 | **Keep the March 1 start date, but focus March–April patrols on known leatherback nesting sites.** | Leatherbacks are endangered and nest early (56 nests in 2025). Most retrofit costs are one-time and don't depend on season length. | Neutral. Targeted patrols cost less than blanket enforcement. |
| 7 | **Manage night beach access instead of closing beaches**: red-light-only rules, guided turtle walks, and education at access points. | Broward allows limited night access, and nightlife is part of its tourism economy. | Neutral to positive. |
| 8 | **Make turtle protection part of the tourism offer**: certify turtle-friendly hotels, run permitted hatchling-release or turtle-watch programs, and market them. | About 3,600 visitors per nest. Even a 0.1% rise in visitor spending is worth about $8M in GDP. | Likely positive. |
| 9 | **Set a common lighting standard across beach cities**, based on the state's model ordinance. | 6 different city ordinances mean more compliance complexity for hotel chains and property managers. | Lower administrative costs. |
| 10 | **Schedule beach work around both nesting and tourist seasons**: require morning nest surveys before any raking, and plan renourishment for the November–February gap where possible. | Nesting runs March–October, but Broward's peak tourist season is winter, so the trade-off has to be planned project by project. | Small project costs. Avoids nest losses and fines. |
| 11 | **Measure results**: map reports of disoriented hatchlings to specific addresses, and collect Melbourne-style crawl data on Broward beaches. | No dataset currently links Broward's rules to nesting outcomes. | Low. It would show which rules actually work. |

## Where to start

1. **Recommendations 3, 5 and 4:** the lowest cost and fastest to put in place.
2. **Recommendations 1 and 2:** the biggest conservation gain for the money.
3. **Recommendation 11:** run it alongside the others to check whether they're working.
4. **Recommendations 8 and 9:** longer-term ways to increase the economic benefit.

## Caveats

- The cost figures rest on assumed per-property costs ($1K, $5K and $25K scenarios).
- Broward's nesting success is already higher than Brevard's (44% vs. 35%), but that gap mostly reflects species mix, not the rules.
- Before adopting any of these, check each city's ordinance text and confirm with Broward's tourism agency which tourism revenue can legally be spent on what.
